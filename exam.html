ITEM 1

Kapeeka Farmers Association have submitted their concern to Nakaseke District agricultural officer about a certain disease that has attacked their cassava plantations and it has the following characteristics: rolled leaves, rotten tubers, stunted growth. The farmers have been invited to be trained at the District farm Institute.

TASK
a) Identify the plant structures and processes affected by these conditions.
b) Explain the effects of the processes to the plants.
c) Explain how the plant manages to overcome these challenges.
Model Answers (Marking Guide)

a) Plant structures and processes affected

Structures:

  • Leaves (leaf blade, stomata) – rolled leaves
  • Tubers (storage roots) – rotten tubers
  • Stems and growing points (apical meristems, internodes) – stunted growth

Processes:

  • Photosynthesis
  • Transpiration
  • Absorption of water and mineral salts
  • Translocation of manufactured food
  • Storage of starch
  • Cell division, elongation and differentiation (growth)

b) Effects of the processes to the plants

  • Photosynthesis: Rolled leaves reduce the leaf surface exposed to sunlight and may damage chloroplasts, lowering the rate of photosynthesis. This reduces glucose production, leading to energy deficiency and starvation of the plant.
  • Transpiration: Leaf rolling reduces stomatal exposure, limiting transpiration. This impairs the transpiration stream, reducing the uptake and transport of water and mineral salts, and can cause overheating.
  • Absorption: Rotten tubers destroy root hairs and cortical tissues, drastically reducing the surface area for water and mineral absorption. This causes water deficit, leading to wilting and leaf rolling.
  • Translocation: Rotting damages vascular tissues (xylem and phloem) inside the tubers, disrupting the movement of water and dissolved sugars. Sugars may accumulate in leaves (feedback inhibition), further slowing photosynthesis and stunting growth.
  • Storage: Decayed tubers lose their ability to store starch. The plant’s food reserves are depleted, reducing the energy available for repair, regrowth and yield formation.
  • Growth (cell division and elongation): With limited water, minerals and carbohydrates, cell division and elongation at the shoot and root tips are inhibited, resulting in overall stunted growth and poor canopy development.

c) How the plant manages to overcome these challenges

  • Leaf rolling (water conservation): By rolling its leaves, the plant traps moist air close to the stomata and reduces direct exposure to wind and sun. This lowers the transpiration rate, helping to conserve water when roots are damaged and absorption is low.
  • Compartmentalisation (walling-off): The plant may deposit suberin (corky material) and lignin around the rotten areas of the tubers. This seals off the infected tissue, preventing the spread of rot pathogens to healthy parts.
  • Chemical defences: Cells around the infection site produce antimicrobial substances such as phytoalexins, phenolics and pathogenesis-related proteins. These inhibit the growth of the pathogens causing the tuber rot, slowing the disease.
  • Activation of dormant buds: When the main shoot is stunted or damaged, dormant axillary buds on the lower stem can be stimulated to sprout. This produces new branches and leaves, restoring some capacity for photosynthesis and growth.
  • Adventitious root formation: In some cases, cassava stems can produce adventitious roots above the rotten tubers when in contact with moist soil. These new roots help regain water and mineral absorption, partially compensating for the lost root system.
  • Resource reallocation: The plant may redirect any remaining stored starch from less-affected tissues to support the growth of new shoots or initiate the formation of small replacement tubers, ensuring some survival and yield.
